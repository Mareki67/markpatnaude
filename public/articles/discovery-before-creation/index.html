Discovery Before Creation

A case for using AI to understand before using it to produce.

By Mark Patnaude • September 2026 • Original Essay

Much of the conversation about artificial intelligence begins with creation.

Write this. Build that. Summarize this document. Generate an image. Create a presentation. Rewrite a résumé. Develop a marketing plan.

Those capabilities are useful. But they may also encourage us to begin in the wrong place.

Before asking AI to create something, there is often a more important question: Do we understand what should be created in the first place?

That distinction matters because creating the wrong thing faster is not necessarily progress.

Creation is downstream of understanding

Consider how organizations traditionally approach a market.

A company should not begin by designing a product and only afterward ask whether customers need it. It studies the market. It examines customers, competitors, demand, pricing, alternatives and unmet needs.

Only then does creation begin.

The same principle applies to many of the decisions where we are now introducing AI. Yet our use of AI frequently reverses the sequence.

We start with the output.

Create the résumé. Write the business plan. Develop the communication. Generate the strategy. Produce the content.

AI makes those tasks remarkably easy. What it does not automatically answer is whether we have adequately understood the environment in which that output must succeed.

Use AI to discover before you use AI to create. Creation follows understanding.

One data point is not a market

I encountered this problem while exploring how AI could be used in career decision-making.

The common approach is straightforward. Find a job posting, give it to an AI system, compare it with a résumé and ask the system to improve the application.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that.

But one job posting tells you about one opportunity.

A body of job postings can tell you about a market.

That changes the questions we should ask.

What requirements appear repeatedly? Which capabilities are actually required and which are merely preferred? Do supposedly similar job titles represent the same kind of work? Are certain capabilities concentrated in particular industries? Are credentials consistently requested, or do they appear only occasionally? Does geography change the market? Where does someone's demonstrated experience already align, and where are the genuine gaps?

Those are discovery questions, not creation questions.

AI can help examine enough information to make those questions practical. It can organize postings, classify requirements, compare terminology and identify patterns that are difficult to see while considering opportunities individually.

The output is not yet a résumé.

The output is understanding.

Only after that analysis should someone decide whether the appropriate response is to reposition existing experience, develop a capability, pursue a credential, change the target market — or perhaps do nothing at all.

That last possibility matters. Good research does not have to produce an action. Sometimes the evidence tells us that our original assumption was wrong.

The principle travels

The same reasoning extends well beyond careers.

Imagine someone considering a new small business.

AI can certainly help create a company name, logo, website, marketing copy and business plan. Within hours, someone can produce artifacts that once required days or weeks.

But those are downstream activities.

What happens if AI is used first to investigate the market?

Who are the likely customers? What alternatives already exist? How crowded is the market? How are competitors positioning themselves? What do customers appear to value? What geographic differences exist? Where might an unmet need actually exist?

Now AI is serving a different role.

It is not primarily the creator. It is helping the human investigate.

The eventual website, business plan or marketing message may be better precisely because it was not the first thing created.

Technology leadership presents the same challenge.

Organizations regularly encounter a promising technology and immediately begin asking how to implement it.

But implementation may not be the first decision.

What problem are we solving? What evidence tells us the problem matters? What capability already exists? What assumptions are we making? What changes for the people who will use or support it? What happens if we choose not to implement it?

Again, discovery precedes creation.

AI as analyst, not oracle

There is an important limitation to this approach.

AI can be wrong.

It can misclassify information, overgeneralize from weak evidence, miss context and identify patterns that disappear under closer examination. A large dataset processed badly does not become reliable merely because AI was involved.

That means discovery requires validation.

When AI identifies a pattern, we should be able to return to the underlying evidence and ask whether the pattern is actually there.

When it classifies something as a requirement, inspect the examples. When it claims a trend, ask how frequently that trend appears and whether the dataset supports the conclusion. When two pieces of evidence conflict, investigate rather than asking AI to smooth over the disagreement.

AI identifies patterns. Human judgment determines what those patterns mean.

Better tools do not eliminate the need for judgment. They increase the amount of information we can bring to that judgment.

Understanding changes what we create

This is where discovery becomes consequential.

If the evidence confirms our original assumptions, we can create with greater confidence.

If it contradicts them, we have learned something before investing heavily in the wrong direction.

A job seeker might discover that the credential assumed to be essential rarely appears in the market they actually want to enter.

An entrepreneur might discover that the service they planned to emphasize is already heavily commoditized.

A technology leader might discover that the organization's problem is not a missing technology at all, but a process, governance or capability problem.

Those discoveries affect what happens next.

Sometimes they change what we create. Sometimes they change how we create it. And sometimes they tell us not to create it at all.

That is a valuable outcome.

A different AI habit

Generative AI has made creation extraordinarily accessible.

That is worth recognizing. But perhaps one of the most useful habits we can develop is to resist beginning there.

Before asking, “What can AI make for me?” ask, “What can AI help me understand?”

Use it to explore.

Use it to compare.

Use it to find patterns.

Use it to challenge assumptions.

Use it to expose questions you did not know you needed to ask.

Then validate what it found.

Then decide.

And only then create.

Because the speed at which we can produce something matters considerably less if we have not first determined whether it is the right thing to produce.

Creation follows understanding.
