Before You Deploy Microsoft Copilot, Answer These Seven Governance Questions

AI does not replace governance. It amplifies whatever governance already exists.

By Mark Patnaude • July 30, 2026 • Originally published on LinkedIn

Over the past year, the conversation around Microsoft Copilot has shifted dramatically. Organizations are no longer asking whether artificial intelligence belongs in the workplace. The discussion has moved to how quickly it can be deployed and how much productivity it can unlock.

Those are important questions, but they're not the first questions leaders should be asking.

The first question isn't whether your organization is ready for Copilot.

It's whether your organization is ready to govern it.

Artificial intelligence doesn't replace governance. It amplifies whatever governance already exists. Strong governance creates trustworthy outcomes. Weak governance exposes inconsistent processes, poor data management, and unclear accountability much faster than traditional systems ever could.

Before expanding Copilot across the organization, executive leaders should be able to answer seven governance questions with confidence.

Question 1 Is our data ready for Copilot?

Copilot works with the information your organization already owns and the permissions you've already established. It doesn't distinguish between information that is current and information that no longer provides business value.

If your governance is strong, Copilot benefits from it. If it isn't, Copilot simply exposes those weaknesses faster.

Early in an enterprise information governance initiative, I partnered with business leaders to challenge a common assumption: that every existing record should simply be carried forward into the future.

Instead, we asked a different question:

Does this information still provide legitimate business value?

Over time, the organization had accumulated outdated customer records, duplicate information, applicant records containing personally identifiable information (PII), and other data that no longer served a legitimate business purpose.

Rather than carrying decades of information forward, we established clear data classifications, reviewed retention requirements, and identified information that no longer supported the business.

The objective wasn't simply to reduce the volume of data. It was to strengthen governance by ensuring the information we retained was intentional, relevant, appropriately managed, and aligned with the organization's future.

Copilot makes that same discipline even more important. It doesn't determine whether information is valuable or current. It surfaces information based on existing permissions and governance decisions.

The quality of its responses will always reflect the quality of the organization's information governance.

Question 2 Do employees have the right access, not simply more access?

Copilot respects the permissions your organization has already established. It doesn't decide who should have access to information; it simply works within the identity and access model you've created.

That makes identity governance a business responsibility, not just an IT responsibility.

During a cross-functional governance initiative, I partnered with Human Resources and business leaders to redesign employee onboarding, offboarding, and role-change processes.

The objective wasn't simply to automate account provisioning or remove access when someone left the organization. It was to establish consistent governance over who should have access to information, when that access should change, and who was responsible for approving those decisions.

As employees changed roles, departments evolved, and organizational responsibilities shifted, access that was once appropriate didn't always remain appropriate.

By standardizing these governance processes, we strengthened accountability, reduced unnecessary access, and ensured identity changes consistently reflected business responsibilities.

Copilot makes those governance decisions even more significant. It doesn't evaluate whether someone should have access to information. Copilot simply honors the permissions that already exist.

The question isn't whether Copilot can find information. It's whether employees should have access to that information in the first place.

Question 3 Who owns Copilot decisions?

One of the quickest ways for a Copilot deployment to lose momentum is assuming it belongs to IT.

Technology makes Copilot possible. Business leadership determines how Copilot creates value.

Technology teams deploy platforms, but they shouldn't decide how Copilot influences hiring, customer interactions, financial decisions, regulatory obligations, or organizational policy. Those decisions belong to the business.

During a major enterprise transformation involving multiple business functions, one lesson became clear: successful governance wasn't achieved because one department made every decision. It succeeded because the right decisions were owned by the right people.

Technology evaluated platforms and implementation options. Business leaders defined operational requirements. Finance established financial controls. Human Resources shaped workforce processes. Executive leadership balanced priorities, investment, and organizational change.

Copilot requires that same governance discipline. It isn't an IT initiative that happens to affect the business. It's an enterprise capability that depends on business ownership, executive sponsorship, and clearly defined decision rights.

Without clearly defined decision ownership, organizations risk inconsistent policies, conflicting expectations, and fragmented governance.

Before expanding Copilot across the enterprise, organizations should clearly define who owns AI policy, acceptable use, risk decisions, oversight, and ongoing governance.

Question 4 What information should never be used by Copilot?

Not every document should be equally discoverable simply because it exists.

Copilot works within the document governance decisions your organization has already established. If sensitive information is stored in the wrong location, classified inconsistently, or accessible to broader audiences than intended, Copilot doesn't distinguish between information that should be broadly shared and information that requires greater protection.

That makes document governance far more than an exercise in organizing files. It becomes a critical component of successful Copilot governance.

During a document management modernization initiative, I partnered with business leaders to rethink how organizational information should be managed rather than simply migrating documents into a new repository.

Together, we established governance around how information should be organized, classified, secured, retained, and accessed based on its business purpose and sensitivity.

Financial records, operational documents, employee information, and other confidential content required different governance than general business information.

The objective wasn't simply to improve document management. It was to ensure employees could quickly access the information they needed while protecting information that required greater stewardship.

Copilot makes those governance decisions more important than ever. It doesn't determine which documents require greater protection or broader visibility. It simply honors the classifications, permissions, and governance decisions already in place.

The question isn't whether Copilot can summarize a document. It's whether that document should have been available to summarize in the first place.

Question 5 How will we measure business value?

One of the easiest mistakes organizations make is measuring the success of a Copilot deployment instead of measuring the success of the business.

Many organizations celebrate implementation metrics:

  • Licenses assigned
  • Active users
  • Number of prompts submitted

Those metrics describe activity. They don't describe value.

Executives should ask different questions.

  • Has Copilot reduced the time required to complete critical business processes?
  • Has it improved service quality?
  • Has it accelerated employee onboarding?
  • Has it reduced organizational risk?
  • Has it improved the quality and consistency of business decisions?
  • Has it allowed employees to spend more time solving problems instead of searching for information?

Those are measures of organizational performance, not technology adoption.

Copilot doesn't create business value because employees use it. It creates business value when it helps the organization perform better.

Organizations don't realize value because they deploy Copilot. They realize value because Copilot improves organizational performance.

It should be measured by the outcomes it enables, the decisions it improves, and the value it creates for the organization.

Question 6 Are employees prepared to use Copilot responsibly?

Successful Copilot adoption depends on more than teaching employees how to use Copilot.

It depends on helping them understand when Copilot should support their work and when human judgment should remain the final authority.

Organizations often focus training on prompts, features, and productivity tips. Those skills are important, but they address only part of responsible Copilot adoption.

Employees also need guidance on when AI-generated information should be verified, when confidential matters require greater discretion, and when business decisions require experience, context, or regulatory considerations that extend beyond AI's capabilities.

The objective isn't to discourage employees from using AI.

It's to help them apply it appropriately.

Copilot can accelerate work. It cannot assume accountability for the decisions people make.

Organizations that prepare employees to exercise judgment, not simply operate the technology, are far more likely to realize Copilot's benefits while reducing organizational risk.

Question 7 How will Copilot governance evolve?

Deploying Copilot isn't the end of the governance journey. It's the beginning.

Microsoft continues to expand Copilot's capabilities across Microsoft 365, introducing new features, deeper integrations, and new ways for employees to interact with organizational information.

As those capabilities evolve, the governance decisions surrounding Copilot must evolve with them.

Organizations should regularly review information governance, access models, acceptable use policies, business ownership, and the measures used to evaluate business value.

Governance shouldn't remain static while the technology continues to mature.

Successful organizations won't treat Copilot governance as a project completed during deployment. They'll treat it as an ongoing leadership discipline that evolves alongside the platform.

Copilot will continue to evolve. Leadership's responsibility to govern it will continue to evolve as well.
